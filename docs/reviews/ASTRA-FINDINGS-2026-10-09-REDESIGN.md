## Executive recommendation

Build a portfolio around Kevin's combination of program operations, developer communities, and hands-on software development. Put specific work, personal contribution, and useful artifacts ahead of atmosphere.

The current site contains credible experience and working products, but the illustrated scenery, startup collage, repetitive italic accents, tiny labels, and elaborate reveals create a generic presentation. This is an editorial judgment about the combined effect, not a claim that a particular asset or paragraph was generated by AI.

A modern result should be easy to read, obviously personal, and quick to navigate. Preserve useful content and existing project screenshots. Reconsider every decorative layer rather than replacing the present template with another fashionable template.

Recommended fresh-start direction: **Working Index**, a distinctive typographic portfolio with a compact identity column and a substantial work column. A quieter **Personal Editorial** direction and a more expressive **Project Studio** direction are alternatives. These are proposals, not an approved final design.

## Evidence and limits

On October 9, inspected the live desktop hero, About section, case-study entrance, and project gallery. Read current components, content data, styles, layout, contact action, and repository guidance. Fetched origin/main before publishing this document; it remains at the baseline above.

The current tree already contains the September review. This document extends its design strategy and fresh-start direction. It does not claim that the September test runs were repeated.

Source checks reconfirm:

- Project cards attach click handlers to article elements without native keyboard controls.

- The hero uses a roughly 9.4 MB video asset and two video elements for crossfading.

These are source findings. No new full accessibility audit, contact-delivery test, Lighthouse run, mobile Safari test, or production deployment-SHA verification was completed. File size is not measured network transfer. Live appearance matched the relevant source, but visual inspection alone does not verify deployment identity.

## Goals and audience

Primary audience: hiring managers and collaborators assessing program operations, community systems, developer relations, onboarding, and related roles.

Secondary audience: people evaluating Kevin's independent product work and ability to turn a problem into a useful tool.

A visitor should quickly understand:
1. What Kevin does.
2. Which work demonstrates it.
3. What Kevin personally contributed.
4. Whether a project is live, experimental, or archived.
5. How to view a resume or get in touch.

Position program delivery and operational systems prominently. The Google accelerator story should not lead primarily with catering, hotels, and flights; those details belong within the fuller delivery story. Do not inflate ownership beyond the actual role.

## What to keep, remove, and develop

### Keep
- The combination of professional program work and independent software.
- Blue42 and Catchr as featured examples.
- The personal motivation behind the software projects.
- Published developer stories as independently inspectable work.
- A restrained warm palette if it suits the chosen direction.
- Clear resume and contact access.

### Remove or reduce
- Full-screen bridge video and the full-screen startup collage transition.
- Repeated orange italic headings, Featured badges, and availability indicators.
- Decorative pseudo-metrics such as XFN, E2E, or an isolated downward arrow.
- Whole-interface thumbnails too small to explain anything.
- Repeated biographical statements in the hero and About.
- The phrase figuring out what to build next.

### Develop
- Two or three professional case studies.
- Specific explanations of difficult product decisions.
- A short personal introduction and, if available, a real photo.
- Clear project statuses.
- A consistent, accessible route from preview to detail to contact.

Do not add a chatbot, fake terminal, skill-percentage bars, gratuitous bento grid, animated cursor, activity feed, or new CMS merely to make the redesign feel technical.

## Fresh-start visual concepts

### Working Index — recommended

A compact identity rail paired with a broad work column. Large, confident sans-serif title; plain sentence-case navigation; forest or ink accent; thin structural rules; purposeful numbered entries only where they help navigation. On mobile the rail becomes a normal top introduction, not a persistent sidebar.

Program stories appear as clear rows with role and scope. Product work gets a larger image and a concise account of the problem. The layout feels like a working professional's portfolio rather than a product landing page.

Why it fits: it connects the operator and builder sides of Kevin's background, supports fast scanning, and makes adding future work straightforward.

Risk: excessive metadata or overly small type could make it feel like an administrative dashboard. Keep text readable and include personality in the copy and artifacts.

### Personal Editorial

A warm, quieter portfolio organized around a direct introduction, selected stories, and a modest personal note. Serif is used as normal editorial typography, without repeated orange italic emphasis. A real portrait could be included later; do not invent or substitute a stock person.

Work previews have enough room to explain a decision. Published writing becomes a natural part of the page.

Why it fits: approachable and useful for community, education, and program roles.

Risk: if filled with vague prose and decorative lifestyle photography, it could become another generic personal-brand site.

### Project Studio

A bold masthead, a concise introduction, and generous screenshot-led project entries. A restrained cool accent and more graphic composition distinguish it from the existing cream/orange site. Each project has a clear role and motivation. Program work remains visible rather than being displaced by app visuals.

Why it fits: strongest for emphasizing current building ability and product execution.

Risk: may position Kevin as a full-time product designer or engineer if professional program work becomes too secondary. Do not make unsupported role claims.

The concepts must differ in composition, hierarchy, and voice, not just colors. The accompanying in-conversation previews are exploratory homepage studies using existing project imagery and proposed copy; they are not shipped pages or new evidence of outcomes.

## Content brief

### Introduction

Lead with a clear relationship between program work and product building. For example:

> I run programs and build useful tools.

Supporting copy can name Google via Cognizant, Springboard, and Draper University, then explain the current independent projects. This is draft copy for review. Do not imply that Blue42 or Catchr were built for those employers.

Use a concise availability line. Avoid repeating a long list of job titles throughout the page.

### Professional case studies

Use a consistent story structure:
- Situation and audience.
- Kevin's exact role and boundaries of ownership.
- Constraints and the decisions he made.
- Actions or process changes.
- Outcomes with measurement context.
- One publishable artifact.
- What he learned or would change.

Google accelerator: show the coordination and delivery problem, curriculum/materials work, stakeholder handoffs, and cohort needs. Retain logistical details where they explain complexity.

Springboard: explain what was wrong in mentor experience, what conversations revealed, and how onboarding/support changed. Define any NPS claim with population, sample size if available, and period.

Developer community: connect specific editorial/community decisions to published work. Describe the combined 520K+ channel audience accurately; do not equate it with measured impressions or growth attributable to Kevin.

### Product stories

Blue42: explain the personal problem, a useful workflow, data sources, an important reliability or evaluation decision, and the current product status. Show the actual UI at readable scale.

Catchr: show save listing → assess fit → prepare for an interview. Explain the scope Kevin owned and the constraints of the browser-extension workflow.

For other projects, include only those that add a distinct capability or story. Mark experimental, private, or archived projects accurately. No invented user counts, adoption, testimonials, or outcomes.

### Metric rules

- 520K+ means channel audience unless reach data supports a different statement.
- NPS 0 → 100 needs clear population and period; include sample size when available.
- 4.9/5 should name the rated experience.
- XFN and E2E belong in descriptive copy, not numeric metric slots.
- If evidence is unavailable, use an accurate qualitative description rather than invented precision.

### Contact

Use a concrete invitation. Keep LinkedIn and a resume easy to find. Publish a direct email link only if Kevin chooses that contact address for public use. Label a PDF link View resume if it opens rather than forces a download. Avoid adding a scheduling service without a reason.

## Visual system and responsive behavior

- Use one primary text family, optionally one editorial family where the concept warrants it.
- Body text: approximately 16–18px, normal weight, comfortable line height.
- Card descriptions: approximately 14–16px.
- Secondary labels: approximately 12–13px; never shrink important information to fit.
- Hero: responsive 44–72px range as a starting point, tuned to actual line lengths.
- Keep ordinary paragraphs around 55–70 characters wide.
- Use color sparingly for actions and identity, with verified contrast.
- Prefer spacing and alignment to repeated boxes, shadows, and badges.
- Show readable screenshots with concise captions; allow larger inspection when useful.
- Use actual project assets. Do not synthesize fake product screens, quotes, or proof.
- Mobile: normal document flow, stacked columns, full-width useful imagery, readable labels, comfortable tap targets.
- No forced one-viewport hero, scroll capture, or hidden core content.
- Reduced motion: all information remains visible and accessible.
- Support 320px through large desktop widths, landscape, and 200% zoom.

## Engineering work that must accompany the redesign

1. Replace animation-gated case-study rendering with visible server-rendered content.
2. Replace mouse-only project articles with native links/buttons.
3. If retaining dialogs, handle focus containment, background interaction, Escape, focus restoration, and scroll behavior.
4. Handle provider-returned contact errors before reporting success; preserve entered content on failure.
5. Fix hidden navigation focus behavior and proper form label associations.
6. Replace the hero percentage-plus-gap layout with a grid that fits its container.
7. Remove unnecessary hero media and below-fold priority loading; measure actual load behavior.
8. Add individual story routes and accurate social preview assets.
9. Consolidate repeated typography/spacing styles and duplicated responsive markup where practical.

Refer to the September AF findings for detailed reproduction notes. Reproduce issues against the implementation baseline before closing them. A successful build is not evidence that keyboard access or contact delivery works.

## Implementation sequence

### Phase 1: dependable access
Remove scroll gating, make projects keyboard-operable, fix contact error handling, and ensure visible focus and readable contrast.

### Phase 2: content and structure
Choose one visual direction. Implement the new home hierarchy and work-detail structure. Draft the three strongest case studies. Confirm claims and project status.

### Phase 3: visual execution
Apply the typography and spacing system, art-direct existing screenshots, add genuine personal assets if available, and refine desktop/mobile layouts.

### Phase 4: verification
Check behavior, performance, links, resume freshness, and metadata. Ship only the scope actually verified.

Keep implementation PRs focused. No framework or CMS migration is necessary solely for this redesign.
